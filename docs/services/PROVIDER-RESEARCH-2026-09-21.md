## Evidence boundary

Six established providers were reviewed through their own current websites. Their longevity, operating scale, testimonials, and reported satisfaction are commercial credibility signals. No competitor result can be transferred to Lokahi Connect.

## What to borrow for Lokahi

These are design recommendations inferred from observed patterns, not scientifically validated conversion claims.

1. **Make the human relationship visible.** Name Stephanie and describe what she actually does with a learner. List only repository-verified credentials. This makes the service easier to evaluate than general “expert support” claims.
2. **Show the experience.** Describe joining Google Meet, working together with shared material, discussing observations, trying a strategy, and reflecting on what changed. Use only steps supported by the approved practice model.
3. **Offer a small first action.** One primary CTA: “Ask about remote support.” A short adult inquiry can begin a fit conversation without implying enrollment, assessment, or a purchase.
4. **Explain the sequence before asking for data.** Inquiry → Stephanie replies → discuss fit and availability → agree next steps. Confirm only operationally supported response times.
5. **Show actual costs with their units.** If verified in GitHub, separate session length/rate from instructional intake. Explain the intake’s scope accurately; do not imply diagnosis. If source authority conflicts, resolve it before publishing numbers.
6. **Describe feedback concretely.** Explain how the learner and family contribute observations and how the plan is reviewed. Avoid promising a reporting cadence that has not been operationally authorized.

## Emotional experience / “qualia” design hypotheses

Here “qualia” means the visitor’s subjective experience of being understood, oriented, respected, and able to choose. The page cannot infer or promise that experience from clicks.

| Possible visitor experience | Design response | Hypothesis to check through voluntary feedback |
|---|---|---|
| “I have explained this many times.” | Start with a brief question about what the family hopes will become easier; allow optional detail. | A short first inquiry may reduce the burden of retelling a history. |
| “Will my child be judged?” | Refer to strengths, questions, goals, and conditions for participation; describe time to think and opportunities to respond. | Concrete descriptions of respectful interaction may make fit easier to assess. |
| “Online help may be another passive screen activity.” | Explain the actual live, reciprocal interaction and device needs. | A session description may reduce uncertainty about participation. |
| “I do not know what I am agreeing to.” | State what the form does, who receives it, and what happens next. Keep enrollment separate from inquiry. | Clear expectations may support more informed inquiries. |
| “I cannot tell if this fits our budget or schedule.” | Display verified duration, price, availability constraints, and location/time-zone question. | Transparent information may improve fit even if raw submission volume falls. |

Ethical suggestion means inviting the visitor to imagine a concrete, plausible interaction: “Your learner can bring a question, try an idea, and talk through what they notice.” Use this only if aligned with approved instruction. Avoid guaranteed confidence, grade-level gains, cure language, fear of falling behind, invented testimonials, scarcity pressure, diagnostic quizzes used for lead capture, and claims of universal suitability. Do not copy competitors’ learning-style, syllable-type, or phonics-first framing into Lokahi’s instructional stance.

All copy below is proposed wording, subject to repository alignment; it is not a claim that services or workflows have already been verified.

- Next step: short adult inquiry; explicit next-step explanation; direct email alternative.

- Sensitive-information helper: “Please keep this first message general. Do not include evaluations, diagnoses, or other sensitive student records.”

## Contact and follow-up implementation requirements

Collect the adult’s name, email, broad support interest, optional general message, and optionally time zone. Do not require child names, date of birth, school, diagnosis, or uploads. Use an explicit contact-consent statement. A success message must reflect an accepted submission; failed delivery needs a visible retry/email route. Email to stephanie@lokahiconnect.org should contain the inquiry and a short internal follow-up checklist: review general need, reply personally, clarify fit and availability, provide the approved next-step scheduling path, request sensitive documents only through an appropriate later process. Do not silently enroll families in marketing.

Suggested operational evidence before release: labeled synthetic submission reaches the configured destination; the same inquiry is retained only where intended; invalid input and error recovery work; no secrets in client code; keyboard labels and focus work; mobile form remains legible. A form link alone does not prove email delivery.

## Low-maintenance quality review

After initial genuine inquiries, Stephanie can optionally ask: “Was anything unclear about the service or what would happen next?” Record only de-identified themes. Evaluate inquiry fit, clarity, reply burden, and voluntary feedback alongside submission count. These measures test the design hypotheses; they do not establish instructional outcomes. No new tracking or automation is required for the first release.

### Response strategy

- **Say:** “Tell us what your learner enjoys, where support would help, and what you hope will become easier.”
- **Clarify:** “What would make support feel useful and manageable for your family?”
- **Prove:** Approved credentials; a concrete session description; verified service terms; a tested inquiry route; accurate next steps.
- **Do not overclaim:** Comparative superiority, causal gains, guaranteed confidence, representative outcomes from testimonials, or any operational feature not confirmed in GitHub and implementation.
