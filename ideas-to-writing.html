What can help when ideas are easier to explain than to write?

You may have plenty to say and still find it hard to get the message onto the page. Explore what helps you keep an idea available while choosing words, organizing a sentence, or reviewing a draft. If you are supporting someone else, begin with their perspective.

Keep the idea available while working on the writing.

A person may know what they want to communicate but find it difficult to begin, organize ideas, choose wording, spell a word, or review a draft. Several demands can occur together. Ask what the learner notices instead of assuming that a short written response means there is little to say.

A useful starting question is: “What do you want this reader to understand or do?” Depending on the person and task, ideas can be made visible through conversation, a brief typed note, a sketch, selected words, or another familiar communication method. These are possible supports to discuss, not requirements to speak aloud or perform in a particular way.

Access supports and explicit instruction can work together. Keeping an idea visible makes it possible to return to that idea while teaching the relevant vocabulary, sentence relationship, spelling, or revision strategy. It does not remove the need to teach what the learner needs to learn.

Compare what a reader can reasonably understand.

A short message with a real purpose

Imagine someone who wants to change a meeting time. An initial draft says, “Can we change it?” In a conversation about a calendar, the other person might know what “it” refers to. In a new message, the reader may not.

One possible revision is, “Could we move Thursday’s meeting from 10 a.m. to 11 a.m.?” That version identifies the meeting and the requested change. It still does not tell us whether the reader is available, so a reply is needed. The useful question is what the wording and shared context support—not simply which sentence is longer.

The learner could compare the drafts, point to the version that matches their purpose, explain a preference, or suggest another version. If neither fits, revision remains open.

This illustrative example shows how purpose, audience, wording, and context work together. The learner’s own goal guides which examples are useful.

Choose a small, informative next step.

  • Locate the difficulty: ask whether the challenge is generating ideas, organizing them, forming sentences, spelling, recording the message, or reviewing it. Leave room for the learner to describe something different.
  • Change one support: try a visible plan, a sentence comparison, or another agreed response route. Notice whether it helps with this task rather than assuming it will help everywhere.
  • Teach and revisit: explain and practice the relevant concept, then return to the message and consider whether it now communicates what the writer wants.
  • Notice what helps: notice which support helped with this draft, then explore how the idea can be used in another piece of writing.

These options can inform a conversation with a practitioner. If you are supporting someone else, involve them in choosing the goal and avoid repeatedly asking them to reproduce a distressing task as proof.

Choose support for the question you actually have.

Lokahi Connect’s educational therapy and literacy services can address writing alongside reading, spelling, comprehension, and learning strategies. Discuss the scope, how progress will be reviewed, and whether the online format fits.

Writing difficulty alone does not establish dyslexia. IDA’s definition identifies persistent word-reading and/or spelling difficulties and recognizes that written expression can also be affected. A qualified evaluation addresses diagnostic questions using broader evidence. Learn about dyslexia and evaluation boundaries.
