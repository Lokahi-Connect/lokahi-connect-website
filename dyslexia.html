Understanding Dyslexia

Dyslexia affects how a person learns to read and spell words. It is not a measure of intelligence, effort, curiosity, or capacity to learn.

What is dyslexia?

Dyslexia is a neurodevelopmental learning difference recognized as a specific learning disability. Its central features are persistent difficulty reading and/or spelling words accurately, quickly, or both. Dyslexia occurs across a continuum, so its effects and the support a person needs can vary.

The International Dyslexia Association’s 2025 definition explains that dyslexia develops through interacting genetic, neurobiological, and environmental influences. Difficulties with phonological processing, morphological processing, or early oral language are common, but no single profile describes every person with dyslexia.

Because effortful word reading can limit reading experience, some learners may later experience challenges with comprehension, vocabulary, written expression, academic progress, or well-being. These are possible consequences, not limits on what a person can understand or achieve.

Learn more from WABIDA

The Washington State Branch of the International Dyslexia Association shares the full 2025 definition from The IDA, characteristics by age, and an explanation of instruction, accommodations, and modifications.

Dyslexia is not an intelligence problem.

People with dyslexia have the same range of intellectual abilities as everyone else. A gap between what a learner understands and what they can efficiently read or spell is a reason to improve access and instruction—not to lower expectations.

What families and teachers may notice

Common signs of dyslexia

Signs change as literacy demands change. One sign alone does not establish dyslexia; look for patterns that are persistent and unexpected in light of instruction and opportunity.

Before and during early reading

  • Difficulty noticing or producing rhymes and working with sounds in spoken words
  • Difficulty learning letter names and connecting letters with sounds
  • Slow recall of familiar names, labels, or verbal sequences
  • A family history of dyslexia or persistent reading difficulty

Elementary years

  • Slow, effortful, or inaccurate word reading
  • Difficulty decoding unfamiliar words
  • Persistent or inconsistent spelling difficulty
  • Reading fatigue or avoidance as the effort of word reading increases

Adolescence and adulthood

  • Reading that remains slower or more effortful than expected
  • Persistent spelling and proofreading difficulty
  • Difficulty managing lengthy reading or writing tasks
  • Stronger understanding through listening than independent reading may show

Important: This page provides general information, not a diagnosis. Reading difficulties can have different causes and can occur alongside other conditions. A comprehensive evaluation should be completed by qualified professionals using multiple sources of evidence.

Best-practice instruction

Why Structured Literacy?

The IDA uses Structured Literacy for a comprehensive, integrated approach to reading and writing instruction. It builds oral language and explicitly teaches the organization of written language. It is grounded in research, benefits all learners, and is especially important for students who need additional support, including students with dyslexia.

Structured Literacy is an approach, not one program or script. Effective instruction connects what is taught with how it is taught, integrates reading and writing, and responds to evidence of what a learner knows and needs next.

What instruction connects

Written language preserves relationships among multiple dimensions. Instruction brings them together rather than treating any one dimension as the whole system.

Phonology Orthography Morphology Semantics Syntax Pragmatics Reading Writing

How instruction is delivered

Teachers make important ideas visible, connect them within a planned progression, check learning, provide sufficient practice, and adapt with care.

Explicit Systematic Integrated Diagnostic Data-informed Responsive

One approach across tiers of support

The core principles remain consistent. The focus, pace, practice, group size, and intensity change in response to learner evidence.

Tier 1 · Core instruction

Explicit, integrated literacy instruction available to every learner in the general education classroom.

Tier 2 · Targeted support

Additional small-group teaching and practice focused on identified needs while core instruction continues.

Tier 3 · Intensive support

More individualized, frequent, and intensive instruction guided by careful assessment and progress evidence.

Structured Word Inquiry and the Four Questions

Structured Word Inquiry (SWI), named by Peter Bowers and John Kirby in their 2010 classroom study, applies scientific inquiry to written words. Teachers and learners use evidence from meaning, morphology, etymology, and phonology to investigate why a word is spelled as it is.

The Four Questions give teachers a manageable way to contain a word investigation while connecting important dimensions of Structured Literacy. They support precise observation, comparison, explanation, and transfer without reducing written language to sound alone.

What does the word mean here?

Begin with the word in a construction and context. Compare literal, extended, or specialized uses when they matter.

How is the word built?

Investigate its present morphological structure: the base and any prefixes or suffixes supported by evidence.

What related words provide evidence?

Compare members of a morphological family and consult etymological evidence without treating history and present structure as the same thing.

How do the graphemes function?

Study how written forms relate to phonemes and how morphology, word history, and spelling conventions constrain grapheme choice.

↻

At Lokahi Connect, we use these four question areas as a flexible, recursive scaffold—not a mandatory processing order. A teacher may enter where the word, context, learner observation, and purpose of inquiry make the strongest opening, then move among the questions as evidence develops.

How SWI fits: The Four Questions can organize rich word-level teaching inside a Structured Literacy plan. They do not, by themselves, replace explicit and systematic instruction across every area a learner may need—including decoding and encoding, oral language, sentence and text comprehension, written expression, fluency, and sufficient cumulative practice.

Putting the information to work

What families and teachers can do next

You do not need to diagnose a child yourself. Begin by noticing patterns, recording useful examples, and asking what evidence and support are available.

1. Notice and document

  • Write down persistent patterns in reading, spelling, language, schoolwork, or fatigue.
  • Include what the learner can do, which supports help, and examples across more than one task or setting.
  • Share the pattern with the classroom teacher and ask what the school is seeing.

2. Ask about screening and instruction

  • Ask which reading and spelling skills have been screened and what the results mean.
  • Ask what explicit instruction is being provided, how often progress is checked, and what changes when progress is limited.
  • Screening identifies possible risk; it does not provide a diagnosis.

3. Consider a comprehensive evaluation

  • If difficulties persist, ask the school in writing about evaluation procedures and timelines.
  • A qualified evaluation uses multiple sources of evidence and considers plausible explanations—not one score or observation.
  • Families may also seek an independent evaluation; availability, credentials, and costs vary.

4. Preserve access while skills grow

  • Use appropriate read-aloud, audiobook, speech-to-text, extra-time, or other access supports when needed.
  • Accommodations provide access; they do not replace effective reading and writing instruction.
  • Keep expectations for ideas, curiosity, and participation high.
